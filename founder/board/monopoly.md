# Monopoly lens
Score: 4/10 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong
- **There is a small market to start in.** "Women with PCOS and irregular cycles who want something to take to a clinician" is narrow enough to dominate first. General cycle trackers are built around regular cycles, so irregular cycles are a reasonable wedge.
- **Local-first privacy is a stance the incumbents would find hard to copy.** Data stays on the phone, there are no ads or analytics SDKs, health data is never sold, and the AI payload is previewed. A rival that earns money from data or ads would have to change its business model to match. Of the four lens moats, a trust brand is the most realistic one here.
- **The expansion path is clear.** The founder's own plan is PCOS, then other conditions (POTS is built, hair loss is planned), then other life stages. That follows the lens's pattern: own one niche, then move outward.
- **The product is already built and tested**, so the money would go to distribution, not engineering.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **There is no distribution.** The brief says how customers find the app is "not yet decided", and there are no users. The lens counts that as half the business missing. The privacy choices also rule out the usual ad-tracking and attribution tools, so paid acquisition will be harder to measure. Nobody knows yet how the first 100 customers arrive or what each one costs.
2. **It is not 10x better, and nothing stops a copycat.** Fast logging, ranged predictions, pattern insights, a doctor PDF and an AI summary are each incremental improvements. An established tracker could add a PCOS mode and an AI write-up in one release. The brief names no proprietary technology, network effect or economies of scale. In month six, only the brand protects it.
3. **Breadth works against owning the niche.** The founder wants an app that "appeals to women with different problems", which pulls toward five life stages and several modules. That spreads the product toward the crowded general market before the PCOS niche is won. The rule of one experience per user helps, but positioning and marketing can still drift.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- What is the secret: what do you know about women with PCOS that the big trackers have missed?
- How do the first 100 users find you, through which channel, at what cost each? (Open question: **/founder-consumer** for where they look, **/founder-cfo** for acquisition cost.)
- How many women with diagnosed or suspected PCOS can you reach, and what share could you realistically hold? (Open question: **/founder-competitors** and **/founder-consumer**. The brief has no market size.)
- Who else already sells a PCOS or irregular-cycle tracker to this same buyer, and what do they charge? (**/founder-competitors**)
- Why now? Is the reason AI summaries, growing distrust of period-app data, or something else?
- In ten years, what is the moat: a clinician-reviewed evidence brand, a referral channel through clinicians or communities, or something else?
- What is the Pro price, and does it cover store fees and AI cost per user? (**/founder-pricing**, **/founder-cfo**)

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. **Commit to PCOS as the only launch market.** Store listing, messaging and the first channel all say PCOS. Other life stages stay in the product but get no marketing until there is a clear lead in the niche.
2. **Show a named distribution channel with a measured cost**, such as a clinician, community or creator partnership. Run a real test (waitlist or pre-sale) before launch and get an acquisition cost from **/founder-cfo**.
3. **Turn privacy into a provable brand asset.** Get clinician sign-off on the medical wording and keep every privacy claim literally true, so it can be checked.
4. **Name one compounding advantage to build in year one**, such as clinician referral relationships or a recognised evidence-cited brand, and show how it gets stronger with each user.

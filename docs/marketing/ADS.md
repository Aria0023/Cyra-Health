# Solia Health ad kit: pictures and words

Generated by `npm run site:ads` from `site/ads.mjs`. Do not edit by hand: `npm run test:unit` fails when this file and
`site/ads.mjs` differ, when a line is over its limit, or when a line names no file that makes it true.

**This is not legal advice.** The checklist at the end is the set of rules these lines were written to, as the person
who wrote them understood the platforms' published policies. No outside page could be opened while this was written,
so none of it was checked against today's policy text. Have your attorney review the kit and the checklist before any
ad runs, and read each platform's current policy when you place it.

## How to use it

- **The website promotes the app. That is the whole plan** (owner decision 2026-11-26): no ad network code, no tracking
  pixel and no analytics on the website or in the app. These ads link to the website's front page (or to the store
  pages once they exist); what happens after the click is not measured by any ad platform, on purpose.
- **Every line is a public claim**, like the website's. Each row names the app or backend file that makes it true. If the
  app changes, regenerate this file and reread the lines.
- **Pictures** are in `docs/marketing/ads/` as JPEG at twice the named size (a 1080×1080 picture is 2160×2160 pixels),
  light only, drawn from real app screenshots (`site/img/`, example data) with the app's own colours and typefaces.
  They are not part of the website build and are never deployed.
- **The call to action** is the platform's own button. Use "Learn more" while the apps are not in the stores, and the
  platform's download button once they are.

## The rules these lines were written to (not legal advice)

### Meta (Facebook, Instagram): personal attributes
- No line says or implies that the reader has a health condition, is pregnant, is trying to conceive or is in menopause. The lines describe the app (“a health log”, “five life stages”), never the reader’s body.
- No “Are you…?”, “Struggling with…?”, “Your PCOS”, “Your pregnancy”. “Your device” and “your log” are about a phone and a file, not a condition.

### Meta: health and wellness, and reproductive health
- No before-and-after, no body image, no negative self-perception, no promise of a result.
- Meta asks that ads for reproductive health products be shown to adults only: set the audience to 18+ when you place them. That is an ad setting, not a statement about who may use the app (the app’s minimum age is still an open question for the attorney).
- Do not build audiences from health interests or from lists of users, and do not install the Meta pixel or SDK. The website and the app have neither, and a unit test fails if the website loads anything from another origin.

### Google Ads: healthcare and medicines
- No claim to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent anything, and no claim about a medicine. The fertility lines say “estimated” and “Solia Health is not a contraceptive.”
- Birth control and fertility products are restricted in some countries: check the country list before running the life-stages concept outside the United States.

### Google Ads: personalised advertising, health
- Health is a sensitive category: no remarketing lists, no customer match and no similar audiences for these ads, and no line that implies knowledge of the reader’s health.
- Run them on keywords and context instead. The website has no Google tag, so there is nothing to remarket from.

### Apple App Store Review Guidelines 1.4.1 and 5.1.3
- 1.4.1 (medical apps): no accuracy claim is made. Predictions are described as estimates and ranges; “not a contraceptive” and “never a diagnosis” are in the kit.
- 5.1.3 (health data): health data may not be used for advertising, marketing or data mining. The app keeps the log on the device and sends none of it to an ad or analytics service; do not change that to measure these ads.
- Store metadata: no price, “free”, ranking or other app’s name in the name, subtitle or keywords.

### Google Play: health apps and store listing
- The short description makes no medical claim and uses no ranking word, emoji or ALL CAPS.
- The Data safety form and the delete-account address (docs/STORE-SETUP.md sections 2 and 9) must match what the listing says about privacy.

### FTC: truth in advertising
- The kit says “no ad networks” and “no trackers”, not “no ads” and not “private”: the Shop lists products, some through affiliate links, and optional features send what she chooses to a server, so the broader words would say more than the build does.
- Every line is literally true of the build and names the file that makes it true. There is no testimonial, rating, user count, award, “clinically proven” or “doctor recommended”, and no health outcome is promised, so no line needs clinical substantiation.
- Subscription terms (price, renewal, how to cancel) are on the website’s pricing section and in the app before purchase; an ad that names a price must name the renewal too. None of these lines names a price.

### FTC Health Breach Notification Rule
- A health app that shares identifiable health information without the person’s authorisation has a reportable breach, and that includes sending it to an ad platform. Never upload a user list, an email list or app events to an ad platform to target or measure these ads.
- The app has no advertising or analytics SDK. Keep it that way.

### Affiliate disclosure
- None of these ads promotes a product from the Shop. If one ever does, the ad itself must say that Solia Health may earn a commission, clearly and next to the claim.
- In the app each affiliate link is labelled “Affiliate link”; the website says so under “Shop by need”.

### State law
- Washington’s My Health My Data Act, and similar laws in Nevada and Connecticut, forbid geofencing around health care facilities and require consent before consumer health data is collected or shared: do not target these ads by location near clinics, and do not use health data for targeting.

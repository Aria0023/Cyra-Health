# Offers lens
Score: 4/10 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong
- **The pain is real and specific.** Women with PCOS and irregular cycles are badly served by trackers that assume a 28-day cycle. "Bring honest patterns to my clinician" is a concrete dream outcome, not a vague wellness promise.
- **The privacy stance adds value.** Local-first storage, no ads, no third-party SDKs and never selling data lower the perceived risk of logging sensitive health data. That raises perceived likelihood for a buyer who distrusts the category.
- **Low cost to deliver.** It is software that runs on the phone. The only meaningful variable cost is the opt-in AI summary, so a stack of bonuses and a guarantee should be affordable (to be confirmed by /founder-cfo).
- **The product is built.** The bottleneck is the offer and distribution, not engineering.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **The free tier gives away the outcome, and Pro sells features.** The doctor-ready PDF, the predictions and the pattern insights are free forever. Pro is a list of features (AI write-up, practice library, reminders, a richer PDF, backup) rather than a bigger or faster version of the result. If the free product already gets her to the appointment, nothing pushes her to upgrade, and the AI chat on its own is easy to treat as a commodity.
2. **The value equation is weak on time and likelihood.** With cycles longer than 35 days, a meaningful pattern takes months to appear. About 12 taps a day is real effort for a tired, symptomatic buyer. There is no proof yet: no users, no testimonials, and the medical wording has not had clinician sign-off. So the buyer is asked for slow, effortful work with no evidence that it pays off.
3. **The market is spread across every life stage, and nobody knows how to reach it.** The brief leads with PCOS but also serves periods, TTC, pregnancy, perimenopause and menopause. Each of those is a different market with a different pain and a different willingness to pay. No acquisition channel has been chosen, and neither spending power nor reachability has been tested.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- Which single buyer pays first: diagnosed PCOS, suspected PCOS or TTC-with-PCOS? What does she already spend money on to solve this?
- What does she get in her first week, before any cycle pattern can exist?
- What proof can you show by launch: clinician review, a pilot group, before-and-after stories from appointments?
- What would make Pro a better outcome ("walk into your appointment with a clinician-ready case in N weeks") rather than more features?
- What is the guarantee, and can you afford it?
- Market size, spending power and growth of the PCOS segment: open, answered by /founder-competitors and /founder-consumer.
- Acquisition cost and channel: open (/founder-consumer, then /founder-marketing).
- Pro price and whether it could double: open, answered by /founder-pricing on top of /founder-consumer. Budget, store fees and the cost of clinician and legal review: open (/founder-cfo).

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. **Narrow to one buyer** (PCOS first) for launch messaging and the offer. Keep the other life stages in the product, but do not market them yet.
2. **Rebuild Pro around one outcome**, for example "appointment-ready" or "find your triggers", with a named stack, a time-bound promise and a guarantee the CFO signs off on (via /founder-offer).
3. **Close the time-to-value gap**: a useful result in the first 7 days, such as a symptom baseline or a lab summary, and a quicker daily log.
4. **Get proof before paid acquisition**: clinician sign-off on the wording, plus a small real-user pilot that produces testimonials.
5. **Validate demand and price** with /founder-consumer and /founder-pricing before setting the store price.
